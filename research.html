Research

I study how physical noise, limited memories, and probabilistic interconnects shape quantum error correction and fault-tolerant architectures. My work combines mathematical models and simulation, from device noise to network protocols.

1.Heterogeneous quantum network architectures

My current work with Filip Rozpędek asks how sparsely expensive memories and local error correction can be deployed while preserving useful network performance. I study hybrid repeater networks in one and two dimensions, keeping memory coherence, gate errors, link-generation probabilities, node placement, routing, and synchronization explicit. The central question is when additional hardware pays for itself, and when waiting or detours erase the benefit.

2.Asynchronous quantum error correction

Entanglement between network nodes arrives probabilistically, so qubits in the same code block can have different ages and noise histories. I am studying how heralding information, memory age, and measurement timing should enter QEC protocols and decoders. Starting with repetition codes and small CSS codes, this work examines when more general codes, including quantum LDPC codes, justify their connectivity and syndrome-extraction costs in a network. This is an ongoing research direction.

3.Trapped-ion noise and geometry

My trapped-ion work asks how electrode geometry changes electric-field noise when the underlying surface-noise spectrum is held fixed. The preprint develops controlled enclosure laws, derives two-point correlation structure, and connects a billiard-style return spectrum to the field-level Green-function boundary response.

This grew out of my honors thesis, which used finite-element Green-function kernels and billiard/TDA diagnostics to compare open, covered, trench, slot, and blade-like geometries. The result I care about is sharper than distance scaling: changing the enclosure can change the total coupling and even flip its anisotropy.

4.Fault diagnosis and mathematical foundations

A separate graph-state project asks what measurements actually identify the physical repair required after a bond failure. Target stabilizer statistics can become nearly blind to the difference between a severed edge and dephased endpoint memories, while failed-graph stabilizers recover the missing mechanism label. The resulting theory separates physical fault catalogues, retained statistics, measurement-context complexity, Pauli weight, and noisy sample complexity.

Another mathematical direction studies the local geometry of exact erasure correction itself. Linearizing the Knill–Laflamme constraints on the Grassmannian of code subspaces gives a covariance-controlled normal operator, an explicit leakage Hessian, and deformation-theoretic tests of code rigidity.

I have also been studying syndrome records themselves as statistical objects. Finite past–future probability matrices give predictive-rank witnesses for memory in repeated QEC circuits. The circuit contribution must be accounted for explicitly; the resulting framework combines separator bounds, finite-sample rank tests, and conditional model checking on public surface-code records.

5.Randomized numerical linear algebra

RA-FEAST explores whether randomized subspace information can reduce the cost of contour-integral eigensolvers without giving up the accuracy that makes FEAST attractive. The project combines randomized initialization, inexact refinement, error analysis, and sparse numerical experiments.
